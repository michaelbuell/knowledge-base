There is no single recovery program that works for every person. Some people begin with medical treatment or counseling, while others first connect with a support group, church program, sponsor, or peer mentor. Many people use several types of support together.

The purpose of this page is not to decide which program is best for everyone. It is to provide a starting place for finding help.

> The most useful recovery program is one that is safe, appropriate, and helps a person remain actively involved in recovery.

## Professional Treatment

Professional treatment may include:

- Medical evaluation
    
- Withdrawal management
    
- Residential treatment
    
- Outpatient treatment
    
- Individual or family counseling
    
- Medication for certain substance use disorders
    
- Mental health treatment
    
- Continuing care after a treatment program ends
    

**FindTreatment.gov** is operated by the Substance Abuse and Mental Health Services Administration. It allows users to search for mental health and substance-use treatment facilities throughout the United States and its territories (Substance Abuse and Mental Health Services Administration [SAMHSA], n.d.).

Professional treatment may be especially important when someone has severe withdrawal symptoms, repeated relapses, an unsafe living situation, or mental health concerns. More information about warning signs can be found in [[3. Managing Stress and Knowing When to Seek Help|Managing Stress and Knowing When to Seek Help]].

## Alcoholics Anonymous

**Alcoholics Anonymous**, commonly called AA, is a peer-led Twelve Step fellowship for people who want help with a drinking problem. Meetings may be held in person, online, or by telephone.

AA meetings often involve members sharing what alcohol did to their lives, what helped them stop drinking, and how they live in recovery. The official Meeting Guide app and the AA website can help users locate meetings (Alcoholics Anonymous World Services, Inc., n.d.).

AA may be helpful for someone who wants:

- Regular meetings
    
- Peer support
    
- A sponsor
    
- A spiritual Twelve Step approach
    
- Connection with others who understand alcohol addiction
    

## Narcotics Anonymous

**Narcotics Anonymous**, commonly called NA, is a Twelve Step fellowship for people recovering from drug addiction. Its focus is not limited to one specific drug.

NA World Services provides a meeting search for local and virtual meetings. Like AA, NA is based on people in recovery helping one another through shared experience and regular participation (Narcotics Anonymous World Services, Inc., n.d.).

## SMART Recovery

**SMART Recovery** offers a different type of mutual support. Its program uses practical tools related to motivation, urges, thoughts, behaviors, and creating a balanced life.

SMART meetings are led by trained facilitators and may be available in person or online. The organization describes its meetings as free, self-empowering support for people dealing with addictive behaviors (SMART Recovery, n.d.).

Someone may prefer SMART Recovery if they want:

- A science-informed approach
    
- Practical worksheets and coping tools
    
- A program that is not based on the Twelve Steps
    
- Support for different types of addictive behavior
    
- Online or in-person meeting options
    

## Celebrate Recovery

**Celebrate Recovery** is a Christian recovery ministry that addresses substance use along with other struggles that it calls “hurts, hang-ups, and habits.”

Meetings generally include Christian teaching, worship, small groups, and personal testimonies. The official website provides a group finder as well as an online weekly meeting option (Celebrate Recovery, n.d.).

Celebrate Recovery may fit someone who wants recovery support that is directly connected to Christian faith. Its spiritual approach connects with the ideas in [[3. Growing Spiritually in Recovery|Growing Spiritually in Recovery]].

## Choosing a Program

Questions to consider include:

- Is the program focused on my actual needs?
    
- Do I feel safe and respected?
    
- Is professional treatment also needed?
    
- Does the program encourage honesty and accountability?
    
- Are meetings accessible enough for me to attend regularly?
    
- Does its spiritual or nonspiritual approach fit my beliefs?
    
- Does it encourage healthy relationships and personal responsibility?
    

It may take more than one attempt to find the right support. Attending a meeting that does not feel like a good fit does not mean that every recovery program will be the same.

## Reflection

I believe that willingness to ask for help is more important than finding a perfect program on the first attempt. Different people connect with different approaches. What matters is finding support that encourages honesty, responsibility, healing, and continued growth rather than trying to face addiction entirely alone.
