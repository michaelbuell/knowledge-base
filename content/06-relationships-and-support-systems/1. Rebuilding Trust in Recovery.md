Addiction can damage trust in families, friendships, workplaces, and other relationships. A person may have lied, broken promises, hidden substance use, disappeared, mishandled money, or acted in ways that made others feel unsafe.

Beginning recovery is an important change, but other people may not immediately believe that everything will be different. They may have heard promises before or experienced years of disappointment.

> Trust is usually rebuilt through consistent actions, not one apology or promise.

## Begin With Honesty

Rebuilding trust begins by becoming honest about what happened. This does not mean sharing every private detail with everyone. It means no longer hiding the behaviors that directly affect the relationship.

Honesty may include:

- Admitting substance use or relapse
    
- Taking responsibility without blaming others
    
- Being truthful about money and responsibilities
    
- Discussing cravings before they become a crisis
    
- Following through with counseling or treatment
    
- Acknowledging when another person has a valid concern
    

The honesty discussed in [[3. Accountability and Goal Setting in Recovery|Accountability and Goal Setting in Recovery]] can help prevent secrecy from returning.

## Apologize Without Demanding a Response

A meaningful apology should focus on the harm that was caused rather than on quickly receiving forgiveness.

A basic apology may include:

1. Clearly admitting what happened.
    
2. Recognizing how it affected the other person.
    
3. Avoiding excuses or blame.
    
4. Explaining what practical changes are being made.
    
5. Following those words with consistent behavior.
    

The other person may still need time, distance, counseling, or stronger boundaries. Recovery does not give someone the right to demand immediate trust.

## Let Actions Support the Words

Promises may be meaningful, but people often begin trusting again after they see repeated evidence of change.

Examples include:

- Arriving when expected
    
- Keeping appointments
    
- Handling money responsibly
    
- Remaining sober during stressful situations
    
- Communicating instead of disappearing
    
- Accepting correction without becoming defensive
    
- Being present for family responsibilities
    
- Asking for help before a relapse
    
- Respecting another person’s boundaries
    

This is where [[2. Healthy Habits That Support Recovery|Healthy Habits That Support Recovery]] and [[1. Building A Personal Recovery Plan|Building a Personal Recovery Plan]] become visible in everyday life.

## Understand That Families Also Need Healing

Addiction affects more than the person using the substance. Family members may experience fear, anger, confusion, financial stress, or emotional exhaustion.

Family counseling or support groups may help family members communicate, establish healthier expectations, and address the effects of substance use together. SAMHSA recognizes family therapy as a possible part of substance-use treatment and recovery (Substance Abuse and Mental Health Services Administration [SAMHSA], 2021).

The goal is not to place responsibility for recovery on the family. The person in recovery remains responsible for their own choices.

## Trust May Return Slowly

Some relationships recover quickly, while others may take much longer. A few relationships may not return to what they were before.

This can be painful, but attempting to pressure or control the outcome may cause more harm. A person can focus on becoming honest, dependable, and respectful even when another person is not yet ready to trust.

The principles in [[2. Healthy Boundaries and Friendships|Healthy Boundaries and Friendships]] can help both sides of a relationship recognize what is and is not healthy.

## Reflection

I have learned that saying I have changed is not the same as showing it. People who were affected by my past choices may need time before they fully trust me again. I cannot control their timeline, but I can control whether I remain honest, follow through with what I say, and continue becoming more dependable.
