A boundary is a limit that helps protect a person’s safety, health, responsibilities, and recovery. Boundaries are not meant to punish or control other people. They explain what a person will accept, what they will participate in, and how they will respond when a situation becomes unsafe.

> Healthy boundaries do not remove every difficult relationship, but they can prevent other people’s choices from controlling recovery.

## Why Boundaries Matter

Some relationships may have been built around drinking or drug use. Other relationships may involve repeated conflict, manipulation, dishonesty, or pressure to return to old behavior.

NIAAA explains that long-term recovery may require establishing routines away from situations and social networks that previously supported drinking (National Institute on Alcohol Abuse and Alcoholism [NIAAA], n.d.).

Possible risks include:

- Friends who continue offering substances
    
- People who make fun of recovery
    
- Family members who ignore clear limits
    
- Relationships based entirely on partying
    
- People who repeatedly create unnecessary conflict
    
- Someone who expects lies or secrecy
    
- Friends who only contact the person when they want something
    

These relationships may also become personal triggers, as discussed in [[1. Identifying Personal Triggers|Identifying Personal Triggers]].

## Examples of Recovery Boundaries

A boundary might sound like:

- I will not attend gatherings where drug use is occurring.
    
- I will leave if people begin pressuring me to drink.
    
- I will not keep substances in my home.
    
- I will not lie to protect someone else’s behavior.
    
- I need time to calm down before continuing this argument.
    
- I will not lend money that my family needs.
    
- I am willing to talk, but not while being threatened or insulted.
    
- I need to focus on treatment before entering another unhealthy relationship.
    

A boundary should usually include an action the person can control.

For example:

> If alcohol is brought into my home, I will leave the situation and contact my accountability partner.

This is more realistic than attempting to control every choice another person makes.

## Communicate Clearly

Boundaries work best when they are explained calmly and directly.

It may help to:

1. Describe the specific problem.
    
2. Explain the boundary.
    
3. State what action will be taken if the boundary is crossed.
    
4. Follow through consistently.
    
5. Avoid making threats that will not be carried out.
    

Not every conversation will go well. Some people may become angry when a relationship begins changing. Support from a counselor, sponsor, pastor, or trusted friend may help.

## Be Careful With Old Friendships

Not every friend from the past must automatically be removed. However, it is important to honestly consider whether the relationship supports recovery.

Questions to ask include:

- Is most of our time together centered on substance use?
    
- Does this person respect my decision to remain sober?
    
- Do I feel stronger or more vulnerable after spending time with them?
    
- Am I hiding this friendship from my support system?
    
- Does this person encourage growth or pull me toward old behavior?
    
- Can we spend time together in a safe setting?
    

Sometimes a friendship can change. Other times, distance may be necessary.

## Avoid Total Isolation

Boundaries should protect recovery, but they should not become an excuse to avoid every relationship. Isolation can increase loneliness, shame, and unhealthy thinking.

As unsafe connections are limited, healthier relationships should be developed through:

- Recovery meetings
    
- Church
    
- Family activities
    
- Counseling
    
- Peer support
    
- Volunteering
    
- Work or school
    
- Healthy hobbies
    

The next page, [[3. Finding Community in Recovery|Finding Community in Recovery]], discusses ways to build these connections.

## Reflection

Setting boundaries can feel uncomfortable, especially when I do not want to disappoint people or create conflict. However, recovery has taught me that avoiding conflict is not always the same as keeping peace. Some limits are necessary to protect my sobriety, family, responsibilities, and the healthier life I am trying to build.
