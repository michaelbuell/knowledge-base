Stress is a normal part of life, but too much stress can affect sleep, mood, health, relationships, and decision-making. During recovery, unmanaged stress may also become a trigger for cravings or relapse.

Stress cannot always be removed. The goal is to develop healthier ways of responding before it becomes overwhelming.

> Being strong does not mean handling every problem alone.

## Recognize the Signs of Stress

Stress may appear physically, mentally, or emotionally.

Common signs include:

- Irritability
    
- Trouble sleeping
    
- Headaches or muscle tension
    
- Racing thoughts
    
- Difficulty concentrating
    
- Changes in appetite
    
- Withdrawing from others
    
- Feeling constantly rushed
    
- Increased cravings
    
- Loss of motivation
    
- Feeling emotionally exhausted
    

Recognizing stress early gives a person more time to use the strategies included in [[1. Building A Personal Recovery Plan|Building a Personal Recovery Plan]].

## Reduce What Can Be Reduced

Not every source of stress can be avoided, but some unnecessary pressure can be reduced.

This may involve:

- Saying no to additional commitments
    
- Limiting contact with people who create constant conflict
    
- Taking breaks from social media or news
    
- Breaking large responsibilities into smaller tasks
    
- Asking family members for help
    
- Creating a basic schedule
    
- Avoiding high-risk environments
    
- Addressing problems instead of allowing them to build
    

Healthy boundaries will be discussed further in [[2. Healthy Boundaries and Friendships|Healthy Boundaries and Friendships]].

## Use Simple Coping Strategies

Stress management does not need to be complicated. Helpful actions may include:

- Taking a walk
    
- Exercising
    
- Slow breathing
    
- Writing thoughts down
    
- Listening to music
    
- Spending time outdoors
    
- Praying
    
- Talking with a trusted person
    
- Completing one manageable task
    
- Eating and drinking water
    
- Resting
    
- Attending a recovery meeting
    

SAMHSA recommends practical steps such as maintaining routines, caring for physical health, connecting with others, and seeking help when emotional distress becomes difficult to manage (Substance Abuse and Mental Health Services Administration [SAMHSA], 2024).

The routines in [[2. Healthy Habits That Support Recovery|Healthy Habits That Support Recovery]] can make these coping strategies easier to use consistently.

## Avoid Unhealthy Coping

Some responses may reduce stress temporarily while creating larger problems later.

Examples include:

- Alcohol or drug use
    
- Isolating for long periods
    
- Lashing out at other people
    
- Ignoring responsibilities
    
- Gambling or uncontrolled spending
    
- Excessive eating or not eating
    
- Staying awake all night
    
- Replacing one addiction with another
    

It is helpful to ask:

> Will this choice actually solve the problem, or will it only help me avoid it for a little while?

## Know When Additional Help Is Needed

Everyone needs support at times. Professional help may be appropriate when changes in mood, thoughts, sleep, substance use, or behavior continue and begin interfering with daily life.

Possible warning signs include:

- Feeling unable to complete normal responsibilities
    
- Anxiety or depression lasting for weeks
    
- Increasing substance use
    
- Severe isolation
    
- Repeated panic attacks
    
- Uncontrolled anger
    
- Flashbacks or nightmares
    
- Feeling hopeless
    
- Hearing or seeing things others do not
    
- Thoughts of self-harm or suicide
    
- Feeling unable to remain safe
    

SAMHSA advises seeking help when mental health or substance-use symptoms make it difficult to manage work, school, home, or relationships (SAMHSA, 2023).

Help may come from a physician, counselor, therapist, treatment center, pastor, support group, emergency department, or crisis service.

> [!warning] Immediate Crisis Support  
> In the United States, anyone experiencing a mental health, suicide, or substance-use crisis can call or text **988** to connect with the 988 Suicide & Crisis Lifeline. In an immediate life-threatening emergency, call **911**.

## Create a Personal Stress Plan

A simple plan can include:

- My most common stress warning signs
    
- The people I can contact
    
- Activities that help me calm down
    
- Situations I should temporarily avoid
    
- My counselor or provider’s information
    
- The nearest safe place I can go
    
- The point at which I will seek professional help
    

This plan can be added to [[1. Building A Personal Recovery Plan|Building a Personal Recovery Plan]] and shared with the people involved in [[3. Accountability and Goal Setting in Recovery|Accountability and Goal Setting in Recovery]].

## Reflection

I have sometimes waited too long to admit that stress was affecting me. It is easy to believe that pushing through everything is the same as being strong. I have learned that recognizing stress earlier, talking honestly, and asking for help can prevent smaller problems from turning into much bigger ones.
