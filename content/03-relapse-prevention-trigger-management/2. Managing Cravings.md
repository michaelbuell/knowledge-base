Cravings are thoughts, feelings, or physical urges that create a desire to use a substance. They may appear unexpectedly or follow one of the situations discussed in [[1. Identifying Personal Triggers|Identifying Personal Triggers]].

Experiencing a craving does not mean a person has decided to relapse. It means the person needs to pause and use a safer response.

> A craving is an urge, not a command.

## Pause Before Acting

Cravings can create the feeling that something must be done immediately. One of the most useful first steps is creating a delay between the urge and the decision.

That delay might be ten minutes, thirty minutes, or long enough to contact someone and leave the situation.

During that time:

1. Notice what is happening.
    
2. Identify the possible trigger.
    
3. Avoid making an immediate decision.
    
4. Move to a safer location if possible.
    
5. Contact someone who supports recovery.
    

NIAAA recommends recognizing, avoiding, and coping with urges rather than assuming they cannot be managed (National Institute on Alcohol Abuse and Alcoholism [NIAAA], n.d.).

## Remember the Full Story

During a craving, the mind may focus only on the temporary relief or pleasure connected to using. It may leave out the consequences that came afterward.

It can help to remember:

- Why recovery began
    
- What substance use previously cost
    
- Which relationships were harmed
    
- How difficult it was to stop
    
- What progress has been made
    
- What may be lost by returning
    

Keeping a written list of reasons for recovery can make this easier. That list can be included in [[1. Building A Personal Recovery Plan|Building a Personal Recovery Plan]].

## Change the Situation

Sometimes the most practical response is simply to leave.

A person may need to:

- Walk out of a store or gathering
    
- Avoid driving past a familiar location
    
- Delete a contact
    
- Go to a meeting
    
- Sit near supportive people
    
- Remove alcohol or drugs from the home
    
- Stop watching or listening to something that increases the urge
    

Avoiding unnecessary risk is not weakness. It gives the craving time to become less intense.

## Use a Healthy Distraction

A healthy activity can interrupt the thought pattern long enough for the urge to pass.

Examples include:

- Taking a walk
    
- Exercising
    
- Calling a friend
    
- Playing with a child or pet
    
- Cleaning or working on a project
    
- Listening to music
    
- Praying
    
- Journaling
    
- Taking a shower
    
- Eating a meal
    
- Attending a recovery meeting
    

The habits discussed in [[2. Healthy Habits That Support Recovery|Healthy Habits That Support Recovery]] can make these alternatives easier to use.

## Talk About the Craving

Cravings often become more dangerous when they remain secret.

A person can say something as simple as:

> I am having a strong craving, and I do not want to handle it alone.

A sponsor, counselor, spouse, mentor, peer-support worker, or trusted friend may help the person slow down and think clearly. This is one reason [[3. Accountability and Goal Setting in Recovery|Accountability and Goal Setting in Recovery]] is important.

## Consider Professional Treatment

Persistent or severe cravings may require more than personal coping strategies. Counseling can help people develop skills for handling triggers, and approved medications may help treat certain alcohol or opioid use disorders (Substance Abuse and Mental Health Services Administration [SAMHSA], 2025).

Medication decisions should be made with a qualified healthcare provider. Asking about treatment is not a sign that recovery is weak. It is another available form of support.

## A Simple Craving Plan

When a craving appears:

-  Stop and identify the trigger.
    
-  Wait before acting.
    
-  Leave the situation if possible.
    
-  Contact someone.
    
-  Remember the consequences, not only the temporary relief.
    
-  Use one healthy activity.
    
-  Seek professional help if cravings remain difficult to control.
    

## Reflection

Cravings can feel convincing in the moment, especially when life is already stressful. I have found that it helps to avoid treating every thought like a decision. Sometimes the most important thing is to slow down, tell someone what is happening, and give the urge enough time to pass without acting on it.
