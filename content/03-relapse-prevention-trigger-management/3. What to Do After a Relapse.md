A relapse is a return to substance use after a period of stopping or reducing use. It can bring shame, fear, disappointment, and the temptation to give up completely.

Relapse should be taken seriously, but it does not mean recovery is impossible or that every previous day of progress was meaningless. NIAAA describes a return to drinking as a setback that may require changes in treatment rather than proof of complete failure (National Institute on Alcohol Abuse and Alcoholism [NIAAA], n.d.).

> A setback becomes more dangerous when shame is used as a reason to continue.

## Focus on Immediate Safety

The first concern should be physical safety.

Depending on the substance and situation, this may mean:

- Stopping further use
    
- Moving away from substances
    
- Contacting a sober support person
    
- Avoiding driving
    
- Seeking medical help
    
- Calling emergency services during an overdose or serious reaction
    
- Using naloxone during a suspected opioid overdose when it is available
    

After a period of abstinence, tolerance may be lower. Returning to a previously used amount of an opioid can therefore increase overdose danger. A relapse involving opioids should be treated as a serious medical risk (Substance Abuse and Mental Health Services Administration [SAMHSA], 2025).

## Tell Someone Quickly

The instinct after relapse may be to hide it. However, secrecy can make it easier for one episode to continue.

Tell a trusted person such as:

- A sponsor or mentor
    
- A counselor
    
- A treatment provider
    
- A spouse or relative
    
- A pastor
    
- A trusted recovery friend
    
- A physician
    

The purpose is not to invite humiliation. It is to prevent isolation and begin making a safer plan.

This is where the support described in [[3. Accountability and Goal Setting in Recovery|Accountability and Goal Setting in Recovery]] becomes especially important.

## Avoid the “Everything Is Ruined” Mindset

After a setback, a person may think:

- I already failed, so it no longer matters.
    
- Everyone will be disappointed.
    
- I will never be able to recover.
    
- All of my progress is gone.
    
- I may as well continue using.
    

These thoughts can turn one poor decision into a much longer return to use.

A more helpful response is:

> This happened. I need to take responsibility, learn from it, and act quickly.

Recovery does not require pretending the relapse was harmless. It requires refusing to let it become the final decision.

## Review What Happened

Once immediate safety has been addressed, it is helpful to examine what led to the relapse.

Questions may include:

- What happened before I used?
    
- Which emotions were present?
    
- Was I isolating?
    
- Had I stopped attending treatment or meetings?
    
- Was I spending time around unsafe people?
    
- Did I ignore an earlier craving?
    
- Was I becoming overconfident?
    
- What part of my recovery plan needs to change?
    

The pages [[1. Identifying Personal Triggers|Identifying Personal Triggers]] and [[2. Managing Cravings|Managing Cravings]] can help with this review.

The goal is not to make excuses. It is to understand the sequence well enough to respond differently in the future.

## Return to Support

A relapse may be a sign that the current level of support is no longer enough.

Possible next steps include:

- Returning to counseling
    
- Increasing the frequency of meetings
    
- Speaking with a healthcare provider
    
- Considering a higher level of treatment
    
- Reviewing medication options with a professional
    
- Changing the home or social environment
    
- Rebuilding a daily routine
    
- Updating the personal recovery plan
    

SAMHSA explains that relapse is common in substance use disorders and does not automatically mean treatment has failed. Treatment may need to be restarted, adjusted, or strengthened (SAMHSA, 2023).

## Repair Harm Without Making Empty Promises

A relapse may affect family members or other people who have already been hurt by substance use. Apologies can matter, but trust is usually rebuilt through continued behavior rather than promises made during a crisis.

A responsible response may include:

1. Admit what happened.
    
2. Avoid blaming other people.
    
3. Address immediate harm.
    
4. Explain what practical change will be made.
    
5. Follow through consistently.
    

This process will be discussed further in [[1. Rebuilding Trust in Recovery|Rebuilding Trust in Recovery]].

## Reflection

I believe one of the most dangerous thoughts after a mistake is that everything is already ruined. Shame can make a person want to hide, but hiding usually makes the situation worse. A relapse should be taken seriously, but it should also lead back toward honesty, support, and another responsible decision rather than becoming an excuse to give up.
