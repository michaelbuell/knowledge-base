A trigger is anything that creates an urge to return to substance use. Triggers do not always lead to relapse, but they can begin a chain of thoughts and cravings if they are not recognized.

Triggers are personal. A situation that affects one person may not affect someone else in the same way. Learning personal triggers is therefore an important part of [[1. Building A Personal Recovery Plan|Building a Personal Recovery Plan]].

> A trigger is not a failure. It is a warning that a healthy response may be needed.

## External Triggers

External triggers come from a person’s surroundings. They may include:

- People connected to past substance use
    
- Bars, parties, or other familiar locations
    
- Seeing alcohol or drugs
    
- Music, smells, photographs, or objects connected to using
    
- Certain weekends, holidays, or anniversaries
    
- Having extra money or unstructured time
    
- Arguments or stressful events
    
- Social media content that makes substance use appear harmless
    

NIAAA explains that environmental cues connected to past drinking can create powerful urges even after a person has stopped drinking (National Institute on Alcohol Abuse and Alcoholism [NIAAA], 2026a).

Some external triggers can be avoided, especially during early recovery. Others will eventually need to be managed through planning, boundaries, and support.

## Internal Triggers

Internal triggers come from thoughts, emotions, memories, or physical sensations.

Common internal triggers include:

- Anger
    
- Anxiety
    
- Loneliness
    
- Boredom
    
- Shame
    
- Excitement or celebration
    
- Physical pain
    
- Exhaustion
    
- Feeling rejected
    
- Remembering past trauma
    
- Believing that using “one time” will not matter
    

Even positive emotions can become triggers. Someone may have previously used substances to celebrate, relax, or feel more comfortable around others. NIAAA notes that urges can be connected to positive emotions, negative emotions, thoughts, and physical discomfort (NIAAA, 2025).

The connection between difficult emotions and substance use is also discussed in [[Trauma and Addiction Understanding the Connection|Trauma and Addiction: Understanding the Connection]].

## Look for Patterns

Triggers are easier to recognize when a person pays attention to patterns.

A simple trigger record can include:

| Situation              | Thought or Feeling       | Urge Level | Healthy Response                    |
| ---------------------- | ------------------------ | ---------: | :---------------------------------- |
| Argument at home       | Angry and misunderstood  |       7/10 | Took a walk and called someone      |
| Passed an old location | Remembered past use      |       5/10 | Changed route and listened to music |
| Long day at work       | Exhausted and frustrated |       6/10 | Ate, rested, and talked with family |

The purpose is not to obsess over every emotion. It is to notice which situations repeatedly create risk.

## Plan a Response

Once a trigger has been identified, the next question is:

> What can I do when this happens again?

Possible responses include:

- Leaving the situation
    
- Calling an accountability partner
    
- Attending a meeting
    
- Changing the normal route home
    
- Eating, resting, or drinking water
    
- Praying or journaling
    
- Spending time with a safe person
    
- Using one of the strategies in [[Managing Cravings]]
    
- Speaking with a counselor about repeated emotional triggers
    

Planning ahead makes it less likely that the person will have to decide what to do while already overwhelmed.

## Reflection

One thing I have learned is that triggers are not always obvious. Sometimes the biggest risk is not seeing a substance but being stressed, tired, angry, or isolated. Recognizing those patterns has helped me understand that I need to respond earlier instead of waiting until the situation becomes much harder to manage.
